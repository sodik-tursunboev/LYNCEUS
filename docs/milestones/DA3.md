# DA3: deep analysis of detection and false alarms (no new functions)

**Asked by the owner (2026-11-17):** "again deep investigation, not another function: deep analysis and detection." DA3 adds no feature. It measures where the existing detectors miss and where they warn wrongly, on data they were not tuned on, and changes only a setting or a condition the data supports, each with its numbers.

**Status: measured and adjusted (2026-11-17); the ledger is `GATES-DA2.md` (DA3 shares it).**

## Findings so far

1. **Where the misses are** (E1 test split, 29,125 of about 44,000 phishing addresses missed): 75% are ordinary-looking own domains (hacked sites and new domains with plausible names; caught 24.7% bare, 17.2% with a path), 16.5% sit on shared platforms with a path, 8% are short links. Short links look like 0.4% caught only because the test reads the address alone; the extension checks the page a redirect ends on.
2. **The platform family is a limit, not a quick win.** In the train split, 9,000 of the 58,800 missed phishing addresses are Google Docs, Slides and Sites links (3,559), Weebly (3,060), IPFS (999), GoDaddy sites (542), Dropbox (384), Glitch (286). A published Google presentation, a Weebly page or an IPFS file is ordinary shared content at the address level, and the page is an image with a link: neither an address rule nor a page rule can read it. The destination is judged when the person clicks through. On live pages, platform-hosted phishing is mostly flagged by its address already (103 of 119 alive, 86.6%), so the family E1 shows missing is the path-bearing kind.
3. **Every legitimate address in the evaluation data was a bare home page** (121,258 PhiUSIIL, 44,920 Tranco; 89 of the owner's 92 browsing URLs have a path). A path-reading model trained on it learns "has a path" (it flagged 100% of phishing in a test). DA2's deep-link corpus is the repair; the rule audit below uses it.
4. **Domain age was under-weighted, now fitted and confirmed.** Registered under 30 days ago: 34.5% of 519 phishing domains and 0.5% of 377 ordinary long-tail sites. The 7-to-30-day band weighed 0.4, below the 0.45 that warns on its own. On sample A (600 domains, used to choose) a weight of 0.45 raised phishing flagged from 53.0% to 58.3% with no ordinary site added; on sample B (600 other domains, read once) from 54.7% to 62.3% (23 more of 300), with 2 ordinary sites added (12 to 14 of 250): two random-letter names on `.cc` and `.app`, registered 17 and 28 days ago, counted as false alarms anyway. **Adopted:** 7 to 30 days 0.45, 30 to 90 days 0.25 (`rules/intel/scoring.toml` 1.1.0; `docs/eval/age-weights.md`). The samples are fresh phishing-feed entries, which skew young; a legitimate brand-new site would warn too, with the reason shown ("registered N days ago").
5. **File reputation separates cleanly** (DA2, `docs/eval/filerep.md`): 100 of 100 Microsoft programs had no engine flagging them; 89 of 97 recent malware hashes reached 4 or more engines and 84 reached 10 or more. The clean set is Microsoft's own programs, the easiest case: third-party legitimate software with one or two detections is untested here.

6. **The rule audit on honest legitimate data** (DA2's deep-link corpus, 1,640 addresses with paths; full table in `docs/milestones/DA2.md`). As the product runs, 25 of 757 dev addresses were flagged (3.3%). The false alarms clustered in a few specific loose conditions, two of which were narrowed with their cost measured on 88,938 training phishing addresses first (`referrer` parameters are not targets: cost 0; `utm_*` parameters are ignored in credential bait: cost 0 of 558): dev fell from 25 to 19 of 757 (2.5%), and the test half was 2.27% (20 of 883). **A third change was made and removed:** silencing the learned model on "platform domains" matched by suffix and silenced every tenant on a private suffix, which collapsed phishing flagged on E1 by more than half; the verification re-run caught it (my cost estimate had used exact matching, the code did not), and the premise was wrong anyway (`wixsite.com` is a public suffix, so its tenants have registered domains of their own). Three more were deliberately left: `open_redirect` on OAuth `redirect_uri` (the same pattern as consent phishing), `brand_in_subdomain` (two domains behind four addresses), and whole-word login words in `credential_bait` (would lose 183 of 558 phishing hits to fix 1 address). **One change was reverted:** requiring a five-letter term for the repeated-letter look-alike branch would have fixed one Hungarian news site, but that false alarm exists only with the popular list off, and the change stopped catching `ollx`, a real look-alike of the three-letter Polish brand OLX (the project's own fixture failed). The lesson kept: a "false alarm" seen only in an artificial view is not a reason to weaken a rule.

7. **The learned domain model does age** (`docs/eval/drift.md`, `python -m eval.drift`). Same test domains, same calibration (0.4% of legitimate names): a model that has never seen a 2026 feed caught 55.1% of PhishTank's 2026 phishing domains (2,315 of 4,199); the shipped model, trained on those feeds, caught 68.2% (2,865 of 4,199). Intervals do not overlap. On the 2024 data (PhiUSIIL) the two are within 1.6 points (44.2% against 45.8%), and the false alarms on legitimate names are the same (0.4%). About 13 points of the model's reach on current phishing come from retraining on fresh feeds, so it should be refitted from new feeds every few months (`python -m eval.learn_url`, then copy the files only if the measurement passes). Not done now: the shipped model already has the 2026-11-10 feeds. OpenPhish has only 54 test domains, too few to read.
8. **What a phishing author can change cheaply** (`docs/eval/evasion.md`, `python -m eval.evasion`; 15,132 of 44,257 test phishing addresses are caught as the product runs; each is changed once and judged again). Plain http, a `www.` subdomain, a harmless query, a `/blog/2024/` path and a digit on the name leave 98 to 100% still flagged; removing hyphens leaves 91.0% (8,062 of 8,863). **Moving the same name to `.com` leaves 35.0% (5,006 of 14,309): about two thirds of what the engine catches in phishing depends on a cheap ending** (`.top`, `.cc`, `.app`, `.xyz` and the like), which is what the learned model and the host-shape rules read. It is a real signal today, because most phishing does use cheap endings, but it is the one lever that would cost an author nothing. No change was made: a rule that ignored the ending would lose what is real (cheap endings really are over-represented), and the other measures here already cover what a `.com` name gives away (domain age, the page in the sandbox, the file lists). Recorded as a limit in `docs/KNOWN_LIMITATIONS.md`.

## What remains the largest source of false alarms

About 1 legitimate page in 42 still gets a warning as the product runs it (2.4% overall, 3.3% in the least-visited rank band), and 22 of the 39 are the learned domain model on spam, piracy, adult and unusual sites and platform tenants: real, not always harmless, and not fixable by a threshold (DA1's two-level model cost more phishing than it saved). Beyond that the rules are not obviously too loose; finding more would need a corpus of a person's actual browsing, which only the owner's own computer has.

## Not in DA3

New rules, new providers, a path-reading model (it needs the corpus and an owner decision), reading pages in the real browser, linking mailboxes.
